What does it mean for a woman to be free to love, to create, and to shape her own life?

Baltic-RISE explores how women connected to the Baltic States and the wider Baltic Sea region engaged with the legacy of German Romanticism, reinterpreting and challenging ideas of love, femininity, and personal freedom from the fin de siècle to the mid-twentieth century. Moving beyond conventional histories of feminism, the project examines diverse intellectual perspectives and lived experiences of emancipation.

Through archival research and digital humanities, it reconstructs women’s intellectual genealogies and transnational networks using original German-language writings and correspondence.

Our approach: the intimate turn

At its core is the “intimate turn”: an approach that places love, marriage, sexuality, motherhood, friendship, and emotional life at the centre of women’s self-formation and agency. It explores how personal experiences, when articulated and shared, can acquire wider cultural and political significance.

In dialogue with Carol Hanisch’s feminist principle “the personal is political” and Rosi Braidotti’s relational understanding of subjectivity, Baltic-RISE approaches women’s emancipation not as a linear succession of individual achievements, but as a complex, collective process shaped by relationships, cultural differences, and intellectual exchanges.

Elfriede Jaksch1842–1897

Born in Riga, Elfriede Jaksch was a Baltic-German novelist and writer who published under the pseudonym Schack von Igar. Her fiction explores women's emotional lives, unhappy marriages, and the tensions between personal fulfilment and social expectations. Her novel Conflicte (1882) offers a valuable perspective on the emerging debates about female autonomy and the Frauenfrage in the late nineteenth century. She also contributed to Riga’s cultural life and participated in public debates on women's emancipation.

Mia Holm1845–1912

Mia Holm was a Baltic-German poet, writer, and educator born in Riga. After working as a teacher and school director, she pursued a literary career between the Baltic provinces and Germany. Her life was marked by divorce and the loss of a daughter, experiences reflected in her exploration of motherhood, grief, and female autonomy. In Mutterlieder (1897), she transformed maternal love and bereavement into poetic expression, offering a deeply personal perspective on women's emotional lives and creative independence.

Ellen Key1849–1926

Ellen Key was a Swedish writer, educator, and influential thinker in European debates on women's emancipation. Her reflections on love, marriage, motherhood, and education challenged established conventions while questioning aspects of contemporary feminism. Her works, including Das Jahrhundert des Kindes (1902) and Über Liebe und Ehe (1904), circulated widely in German translation. Key also wrote personal correspondence directly in German, notably her letters to Lou Andreas-Salomé (1898–1920), offering valuable original sources for exploring their intellectual relationship and shared debates on intimacy and female autonomy.

Laura Marholm1854–1928

Laura Marholm was a Baltic-German writer and literary critic born in Riga. An influential yet controversial voice in debates on women's emancipation, she examined sexual difference, creativity, and the relationship between intimate partnerships and female self-development. In Das Buch der Frauen (1895), she explored the lives of prominent contemporary women, developing an often ambivalent understanding of female nature, individuality, and intellectual independence.

Monika Hunnius1858–1934

Monika Hunnius was a Baltic-German writer, singer, and music educator born in Riga. Choosing an independent artistic career over marriage, she challenged conventional expectations of women's lives while remaining deeply influenced by Protestant ideals of femininity. Her autobiographical Mein Weg zur Kunst (1925) explores artistic vocation and personal autonomy, while Baltische Frauen von einem Stamm (1930) portrays contrasting models of womanhood, marriage, and female identity. Her writings reveal the tensions between traditional gender ideals and women's aspirations for independence.

Lou Andreas-Salomé1861–1937

Born in St Petersburg into a family with Baltic roots, Lou Andreas-Salomé was a German-language writer, philosopher, and psychoanalyst whose unconventional life challenged prevailing expectations of marriage and female independence. In works such as Die Erotik (1910) and Das Haus (1921), she explored sexuality, intimate relationships, and women's self-realisation. Her correspondence and friendships with Ellen Key and Frieda von Bülow, alongside her intimate and intellectual relationship with Rainer Maria Rilke, reveal the importance of female friendship, emotional bonds, and creative exchange in shaping her understanding of personal freedom.

Aspazija1865–1943

Aspazija, born Johanna Emīlija Lizete Rozenberga, was a Latvian poet, playwright, and pioneering advocate of women's emancipation. After an unhappy first marriage, she established an independent literary career, challenging traditional gender roles through powerful female characters and reflections on love and freedom. Her lifelong intellectual and intimate partnership with Rainis, marked by political persecution and exile, is documented in an extensive correspondence, including original German-language letters. Her writings offer a distinctive perspective on the relationship between personal autonomy, creative expression, and social emancipation.

Helene von Klot-Heydenfeldt1865–1943

Born in Riga, Helene von Klot-Heydenfeldt was a Baltic-German writer whose novel Eine Frau. Studien nach dem Leben (1892), written in response to Tolstoy’s The Kreutzer Sonata, explored marriage, sexuality, and women's emotional experiences. In 1896, she met Lou Andreas-Salomé in Munich, beginning a lifelong friendship that connected her to wider German-speaking intellectual circles. Her work and personal networks offer a valuable perspective on female subjectivity, intimate relationships, and the negotiation of traditional gender roles around 1900.

Elfriede Eckardt-Skalberg1884–1964

Elfriede Eckardt-Skalberg was a German-language poet, translator, and journalist from Latvia whose writing engaged with Symbolism and Expressionism. Her poetry collection Über drei Stufen (1907) explores love, erotic desire, suffering, and disillusionment, questioning Romantic ideals of enduring love. In 1915, she married the writer Guido Hermann Eckardt, whose work included an early Baltic novel addressing homosexuality. Through her poetry and translations, Eckardt-Skalberg also connected German-speaking and Latvian literary cultures.

Elisabeth Goercke1888–1966

Born in Talsi, Courland, Elisabeth Goercke was a Baltic-German poet, pianist, playwright, and translator. Alongside her work as a music teacher, she published poetry exploring love, emotional experience, nature, and belonging, including Viel süße Minne (1912) and Nach den Sternen (1930). Her literary collaboration and friendship with the Latvian writer Haralds Eldgasts contributed to cultural exchange between German- and Latvian-speaking communities. Her surviving correspondence with Zenta Mauriņa offers a further connection to Baltic women's intellectual networks.

Gertrud von den Brincken1892–1982

Born in Courland into a Baltic-German aristocratic family, Gertrud von den Brincken was a poet and novelist whose early life was shaped by family loss, financial hardship, and the upheavals of war. Before marrying the philosopher Walther Schmied-Kowarzik in 1925, she worked as a nurse and teacher to support her family. Her poetry, including Wer nicht das Dunkel kennt (1911) and Das Heimwehbuch (1926), explores love, suffering, faith, and the longing for belonging. Her novels and autobiographical writings reflect the disappearance of the Baltic-German world, connecting intimate experience with cultural memory and historical displacement.

Zenta Mauriņa1897–1978

Zenta Mauriņa was a Latvian writer, essayist, and philosopher who published extensively in Latvian and German. A survivor of childhood polio, she developed an independent intellectual career despite lifelong physical disability. Her writings explore love, suffering, spiritual freedom, and human relationships. Her personal life was closely intertwined with that of Konstantīns Raudive, whose daughter, born outside marriage, she helped raise and financially support. Her extensive German-language correspondence with family members and friends, including her close associate Annemarie Morgenthaler, offers a particularly valuable perspective on friendship, emotional attachment, care, and everyday life in exile. These personal relationships illuminate the connections between Mauriņa's lived experiences and her philosophical reflections on love and human existence.

The digital corpus

In development

Texts and
correspondence.

The Baltic-RISE Digital Corpus is being developed as an open-access collection of selected German-language literary works and correspondence.

Digitised originals will be accompanied, where available and permitted, by verified German transcriptions, selected English and Latvian translations, and contextual annotations in English.
